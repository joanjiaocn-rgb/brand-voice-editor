Guides / Voice guidelines

A usable reference

How do you write brand voice guidelines?

Write brand voice guidelines as a short decision-making tool. Define the reader relationship, three voice traits with boundaries, specific writing rules, words to use or avoid, and before-and-after examples drawn from real work.

The best guideline is not the most polished presentation. It is the document a writer opens while drafting an email, landing page, product notice, or support reply.

What should brand voice guidelines include?

  1. A one-sentence voice statement: who the brand is to the reader and how it communicates.
  2. Three voice traits: each expressed as "we are, which means, but not."
  3. Writing rules: choices about openings, sentence length, vocabulary, evidence, and calls to action.
  4. Tone adjustments: how the voice changes for launches, problems, instructions, and sensitive messages.
  5. Before-and-after examples: real transformations that demonstrate the rules.
  6. A short review checklist: questions an editor can answer before publishing.

Start with a relationship, not a personality

A statement such as "We are bold, human, and authentic" leaves too much room for interpretation. Describe the role the brand plays for a specific reader instead.

We write like an experienced specialist helping a busy peer make a sound decision: direct about the recommendation, open about trade-offs, and never impressed by our own expertise.

This statement gives context to later rules. It explains why the voice is direct, how expertise should appear, and what kind of behavior is out of bounds.

Make every trait observable

Vague guidanceUsable guidance
Be conversational.Use contractions and everyday verbs. Do not add slang or filler.
Sound confident.State the recommendation before the background. Name uncertainty when it matters.
Keep it simple.Use one idea per paragraph. Define technical terms the first time they appear.
Be customer-first.Lead with what changes for the reader, not what the company has achieved.

Use before-and-after examples

Examples reveal how several rules work together. Use ordinary copy your team writes often, and annotate why the improved version fits.

Before: "We are incredibly excited to announce that we have implemented a number of significant improvements to our reporting experience."

After: "Reporting is easier to scan now. The new view groups revenue, churn, and expansion by week."

The revision removes company-centered excitement, leads with the reader benefit, and replaces "significant improvements" with concrete detail.

Who should own the guidelines?

Give one person responsibility for the source document, but let recurring evidence drive changes. When sales, support, and product teams keep making the same exception, the guideline may be incomplete. Review it quarterly at first, then whenever the audience, product, or position changes materially.

Use the guideline in daily work

Add a light review process that catches drift without slowing every message down.
